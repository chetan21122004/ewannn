# From Strangers to Stories: A Journey Through Language 

This was 2012, when I conducted the field survey for my research work on primary education in North Bengal. My field site was none other than the thick and peaceful Gorumara National Park, home to tall trees, distant hills, and wild animals. It seemed all peaceful, but the toughest part wasn't the forest; it was communicating.The local village in which I was to conduct my research consisted of mostly tribal people who used their local language, foreign to me. When I tried asking the basic questions using my questionnaire on the first day, there seemed to be no response as no one understood what I said. It all happened the following afternoon when an elderly man walked up to me. He was able to speak some broken Bengali. This moment was like the opening of a window in a closed room. I started talking in slow Bengali to him, and he translated my queries into their dialect, while they told me back their answers through him in Bengali.All of this happened thereafter.With him around, I was able to communicate with the villagers. They started trusting me and opened up to me about their life, including their problems concerning the education of their children. Something that was beyond reach the other day was easily attainable. On my last day there, the elderly man looked at me and said, “Since you knew Bengali, you were able to understand us.”This sentence haunted me ever since. While exploring the wilderness of Gorumara, I gained a valuable insight—that language isn’t merely about communicating; it’s about forming connections with other humans. That day, speaking Bengali wasn’t only helpful in completing my survey; it saved my life. People who talked about these tribal communities did not approach the issue with any degree of enthusiasm or warmth, rather they seemed wary of them or afraid of them as if they were something untouchable. However, this was completely wrong on their part as I found these tribes to be warm and welcoming. It is only because they were new and we had come from far away that they had put up these barriers. After many years, I received a message out of the blue. It was from one of the children I used to meet while conducting my survey. He was the curious child who used to keep himself close by whenever we were having a conversation. He had turned 22 and graduated. He had written a message inviting me to his home in Bengali.As I read that message, it made me proud and gave me a sense of belonging that had nothing to do with time. The wilderness at Gorumara taught me something invaluable—the power of language transcends mere communication; it’s the means to build trust and connect on a human level.
